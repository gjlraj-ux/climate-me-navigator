Section 1 · 20 min

Why M&E exists (and what it actually is)

By the end of this section you can
  • Define monitoring and evaluation and explain how they differ
  • Name the three core purposes of M&E: accountability, learning, decision-making
  • Describe the M&E cycle and where your role fits in it

1.1 Two words, two different jobs

Monitoring is the routine, continuous tracking of what a program is doing and delivering. It answers: Are we doing what we said we would, on time and on budget? Think of it as the program's dashboard — you check it constantly while driving.

Evaluation is a periodic, structured assessment of whether the program is working — whether it is relevant, effective, and creating the change it promised. It answers: Are we doing the right things, and are they making a difference? Think of it as the mechanic's full inspection: less frequent, deeper, asking harder questions.

Monitoring vs. evaluation at a glance
MonitoringEvaluation
TimingContinuous, throughout implementationPeriodic — midterm, endline, or after closure
Core questionAre we on track?Did it work? Why or why not?
FocusInputs, activities, outputsOutcomes, impact, relevance, sustainability
Who does itUsually internal program staffInternal, external, or mixed teams, chosen for the purpose and required independence
Used forCourse corrections, progress reportsStrategic decisions, scale-up/redesign, accountability

1.2 The three purposes

  • Accountability — to donors who fund the work, and just as importantly to the communities the program serves. M&E is how you demonstrate that resources became results.
  • Learning — finding out what works, what doesn't, and why, so the next phase (or the next program) is better.
  • Decision-making — giving managers timely evidence to adapt: reallocate budget, retrain staff, change an approach mid-stream.
Field note
A common trap: treating M&E as something done for the donor. Strong programs treat the donor report as a by-product. The real client of M&E is the program team itself — and the community whose lives the data describes.

1.3 The M&E cycle

  1. Plan — define the theory of change, indicators, and M&E plan during program design (not after launch).
  2. Collect — gather baseline data, then routine monitoring data.
  3. Analyze — compare actuals against targets; look for patterns and surprises.
  4. Use — report, reflect, and adapt the program.
  5. Evaluate — at key milestones, step back to assess outcomes and impact, feeding lessons into the next planning cycle.
Field practice 1
Think of a program you know well. Write one monitoring question (about delivery) and one evaluation question (about change) for it. Example — school feeding program: Monitoring: how many meals were served per school per week? Evaluation: did attendance and concentration improve among participating children?
"We surveyed farmers 18 months after training to see if yields rose." Monitoring or evaluation?
Evaluation. It looks at outcomes (yield change) after a period of time, not routine delivery tracking.
"We log attendance at every weekly session." Monitoring or evaluation?
Monitoring — continuous tracking of an output (participation in sessions).
Section 2 · 30 min

Theory of change & the logframe

By the end of this section you can
  • Explain a results chain from inputs to impact
  • Articulate a simple theory of change with assumptions
  • Read and contribute to a logical framework (logframe)

2.1 The results chain

Every program is a bet: if we use these resources to do these things, this change will follow. The results chain (the interactive bar at the top of this page) makes that bet explicit. The crucial line is between outputs and outcomes — everything up to outputs is within your control; outcomes depend on people and systems responding. That's where programs succeed or fail, and where M&E earns its keep.

Worked example — community handwashing program
LevelExampleKey question
Inputs$50k, 2 staff, soap supplies, IEC materialsWhat do we use?
ActivitiesRun hygiene sessions in 20 villages; install handwashing stationsWhat do we do?
Outputs1,200 people trained; 40 stations installedWhat do we deliver?
Outcomes% of households practicing handwashing at key times rises from 22% to 60%What changes in behavior/condition?
ImpactReduced incidence of diarrheal disease in children under 5What long-term difference results?

2.2 Theory of change: the "why" between the boxes

A theory of change (ToC) is the results chain plus the reasoning and assumptions that connect each step. Assumptions are conditions outside your control that must hold for the chain to work — and they are where programs quietly break.

In the handwashing example: trained people will only change behavior if soap remains affordable, if water is available, and if social norms don't penalize the new practice. A good ToC names these out loud so monitoring can watch them.

Common pitfall
The "miracle in the middle": a beautifully detailed activity plan, a bold impact statement, and a hand-wave in between. If you can't explain why an output should produce an outcome, you don't yet have a theory of change — you have a to-do list and a wish.

2.3 The logframe

The logical framework is the standard 4×4 planning table donors expect. Rows are the results levels (often labeled Goal / Outcome / Outputs / Activities); columns are:

  1. Narrative summary — what you intend to achieve at each level
  2. Indicators — how you'll measure it (Section 3)
  3. Means of verification (MoV) — where the data comes from (registers, surveys, records)
  4. Assumptions — external conditions that must hold to move up to the next level

Read a logframe diagonally, bottom-up: if activities are done and assumptions hold, outputs result; if outputs are delivered and assumptions hold, the outcome follows — and so on to the goal.

Field practice 2
Sketch a one-line results chain for your own program (one item per level). Then write the single assumption you are most worried about between outputs and outcome. That assumption belongs on your monitoring radar.
"40 latrines constructed" — which level of the results chain?
Output. It's a deliverable fully within the program's control. "% of households using latrines" would be the outcome.
Where do assumptions sit in a logframe, and why do they matter?
In the fourth column, linking each level to the next. They are the conditions outside your control on which your logic depends — if they fail, delivery can be perfect and results can still not happen.
Section 3 · 30 min

Indicators, baselines & targets

By the end of this section you can
  • Write a SMART indicator and pair it with a means of verification
  • Distinguish quantitative from qualitative indicators and when each is needed
  • Explain why baselines and targets make data meaningful

3.1 What an indicator is

An indicator is an agreed, observable signal that something in your results chain is (or isn't) happening. It doesn't prove change by itself — it indicates it. "Number of women completing vocational training" tells you about an output; "% of graduates earning income from the trade 6 months later" tells you about the outcome.

3.3 Quantitative and qualitative — you need both

Quantitative indicators count: numbers, percentages, rates. They tell you how much changed and let you compare across time and sites. Qualitative indicators capture experience, perception, and process — why and how change happened (or didn't), in people's own words. A program that only counts will know it's off-track but not why; a program that only narrates can't show scale. The strongest M&E systems triangulate both.

3.4 Baselines and targets

A number without a comparison point is hard to interpret. Baseline: the value before (or at the very start of) the intervention. Target: the value you commit to reaching by a date. "60% of households practice handwashing" means little until you know it started at 22% and the Year-2 target is 55%. Rule of thumb: no baseline, no defensible claim of change over time. A baseline supports comparison but does not by itself establish that an intervention caused the change. Collect baselines before activities begin — reconstructing them later is expensive and less credible.

Common pitfall
Indicator overload. Ten well-chosen indicators that are actually collected, checked, and used beat forty that exist only in the proposal. Every indicator you add is a recurring cost in staff time and data quality risk. Be ruthless.
Field note · Disaggregation
Always plan to break data down — at minimum by sex and age, and where relevant by disability, location, or wealth group. An average can hide the fact that a program works well for men and not at all for women. Donors increasingly require it; good practice demands it.
Field practice 3
Take the outcome you wrote in Field practice 2. Draft one quantitative indicator (with baseline + target + date) and one qualitative indicator for it. Then name the means of verification for each — the actual register, survey, or record where the data will live.
What's wrong with the indicator "Communities are more resilient"?
It isn't specific or measurable — "resilient" is undefined, there's no unit, no baseline, no timeframe. Translate it into observable signals, e.g. "% of households with at least one alternative income source by end of Year 2."
Your endline shows 65% of farmers use improved seed. Can you report success?
Not yet. Compared to what? Without a baseline (and a target), 65% could be an improvement, no change, or even a decline. This is why baselines come first.
Section 4 · 35 min

Collecting data people can trust

By the end of this section you can
  • Match common collection methods to the questions they answer best
  • Explain basic sampling logic and the five dimensions of data quality
  • Apply core ethical standards: consent, do-no-harm, data protection

4.1 Choosing a method

The everyday M&E toolkit
MethodBest forWatch out for
Routine records (registers, attendance logs, distribution lists)Outputs; cheap, continuous monitoringGaps and over-reporting; verify periodically
Surveys / questionnairesQuantifying outcomes across many people; baseline–endline comparisonLeading questions; survey fatigue; needs decent sampling
Key informant interviews (KIIs)Depth from people with special knowledge (leaders, officials, staff)One person's view ≠ the community's view
Focus group discussions (FGDs)Norms, perceptions, the "why" behind survey numbersDominant voices; group composition shapes what's sayable
Direct observationVerifying that things exist and are used (stations built, practices followed)People behave differently when watched
Secondary data (government stats, clinic records)Context, impact-level trends, saving moneyDifferent definitions, coverage, and quality than yours

The principle behind all of it: triangulate. No single method is reliable alone; confidence comes from independent sources pointing the same way.

4.2 Sampling in one paragraph

You rarely need to ask everyone. A sample is a subset chosen to stand in for the whole. If it's selected randomly (every person/household has a known chance of selection), you can generalize from it; if it's selected for convenience — whoever was near the road, whoever came to the meeting — you cannot, because the people you reached differ systematically from those you didn't. For beginner purposes, remember three things: random beats convenient; bigger samples narrow uncertainty but with diminishing returns; and the people you miss (the absent, the remote, the marginalized) are often exactly the ones the program most needs to hear.

4.3 Five dimensions of data quality

  • Validity — does it measure what it claims to measure?
  • Reliability — would a repeat collection, or a different enumerator, get the same result?
  • Timeliness — is it recent enough to act on?
  • Precision — is the detail sufficient for the decision at hand?
  • Integrity — is it protected from error and manipulation along the way?

4.4 Ethics is not optional

  • Informed consent — people understand who you are, why you're asking, that participation is voluntary, and that refusing carries no penalty (especially no loss of assistance).
  • Do no harm — asking about sensitive topics (violence, income, status) can expose people to risk. Design questions, settings, and interviewer selection accordingly.
  • Confidentiality & data protection — collect the minimum personal data needed, store it securely, restrict access, and never publish anything that lets individuals be identified.
  • Feedback — communities give you their time and their stories; share findings back in a form they can use.
Field practice 4
Your survey says 90% of beneficiaries are satisfied — but the survey was run by the same staff who deliver the program, at a distribution site, with names recorded. List three reasons this number might be inflated, and one change to the method for each.
You interview only farmers who attend the weekly market. What's the problem?
Selection bias — a convenience sample. Market-goers likely differ from non-attenders (mobility, wealth, proximity), so results can't be generalized to all farmers.
Name two ways to triangulate "40 handwashing stations installed and in use."
Cross-check installation records with direct observation spot-checks, and add FGD or survey questions on actual use — records prove existence; observation and self-report speak to use.
Section 5 · 30 min

Evaluation essentials

By the end of this section you can
  • Distinguish the main evaluation types and when each is used
  • Apply the six OECD-DAC criteria as evaluation questions
  • Explain attribution vs. contribution and why baselines/comparisons matter

5.1 Types of evaluation

What kind of evaluation, when
TypeWhenCore question
Formative / midtermDuring implementationWhat should we adjust while there's still time?
Process evaluationDuring or afterWas it implemented as designed, and why/why not?
Summative / final (endline)At or after closureDid we achieve the intended outcomes?
Impact evaluationAfter sufficient time, with a comparison designWhat change is attributable to the program itself?

5.2 The OECD-DAC criteria — the evaluator's compass

Most NGO evaluations are framed around six internationally agreed criteria. Knowing them lets you read any evaluation report — and brief any evaluator.

  • Relevance — is the program doing the right things for these people, in this context?
  • Coherence — does it fit with what others (government, other agencies, your own organization) are doing?
  • Effectiveness — is it achieving its objectives?
  • Efficiency — are results being delivered economically and on time?
  • Impact — what higher-level difference is it making, intended or not, positive or negative?
  • Sustainability — will the benefits last after the program (and its funding) ends?

5.3 Attribution vs. contribution

If incomes rose in your project area, was it your program — or the new road, good rains, and remittances? Attribution means demonstrating the change was caused by your program, which requires a counterfactual: a credible picture of what would have happened without it (e.g., a comparison group). Contribution is the more modest, often more honest claim: evidence that your program plausibly played a part in the change, alongside other factors. For most NGO programs, a rigorous contribution story — strong baseline–endline data, a clear theory of change, triangulated evidence, alternative explanations examined — is both achievable and respectable. Don't claim attribution your design can't support.

Field note · Being evaluated
Program staff often experience evaluation as an audit. Reframe it: the evaluator's report is only as good as the records, monitoring data, and candor you provide. Negative findings documented honestly are more valuable to your organization — and more credible to donors — than glowing findings nobody believes.
Field practice 5
Write one evaluation question for your program under each of three DAC criteria: relevance, effectiveness, sustainability. Make each question answerable with evidence, not opinion.
"The training was completed on budget, but graduates can't find jobs because there's no local demand for the trade." Which DAC criteria are in tension?
Efficiency was fine (delivered economically), but relevance failed (wrong intervention for the context) — and therefore effectiveness and sustainability suffer. Efficient delivery of the wrong thing is still failure.
Yields rose 20% in your area and 18% in neighboring non-program districts. What does this suggest?
Most of the change likely came from external factors (e.g., rainfall, prices) affecting everyone. The comparison suggests your program's net effect may be small — exactly why counterfactual thinking matters before claiming credit.
Section 6 · 25 min

Using data: learning, reporting & the M&E plan

By the end of this section you can
  • List the components of a practical M&E plan
  • Run a simple data-review rhythm that leads to decisions
  • Report results honestly, including what didn't work

6.1 Data that is never used is a cost, not an asset

The most common M&E failure isn't bad data — it's unused data: forms filled, databases populated, reports filed, nothing changed. Close the loop with a regular rhythm:

  1. Monthly — team reviews output data against workplan; flags slippage.
  2. Quarterly — compare indicator actuals vs. targets; discuss the assumptions you flagged in Section 2; record explicit decisions ("we will…") with owners and deadlines.
  3. Annually / at evaluation points — step back to outcomes: is the theory of change holding? What do we change in next year's plan?

This habit has a name — adaptive management: using evidence to adjust course deliberately rather than rigidly executing a plan written before reality had a vote.

6.2 The M&E plan — your system on paper

An M&E plan turns intentions into an operating system. At minimum it contains:

  • The logframe / results framework with final indicators
  • An indicator reference sheet for each indicator: precise definition, unit, disaggregation, baseline, targets, method, frequency, responsible person
  • Data flow — who collects, who checks, who enters, who analyzes, who sees it, by when
  • Data quality and ethics procedures — spot-checks, consent scripts, storage rules
  • Use & reporting calendar — review meetings, donor reports, community feedback sessions
  • Budget — cost the evidence, learning, safeguarding, participation, and evaluation work the decisions require. A 5–10% allocation is sometimes used as an early planning heuristic, but it is not a universal standard.

6.3 Honest reporting

Report against targets, not just activity ("we trained 1,150 of a planned 1,200 — 96%"), explain variances, disaggregate, and include negative results and unintended effects with what you're doing about them. A report that contains only good news is read as marketing; a report that shows learning is read as competence.

Common pitfall
Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it gets gamed. If field teams are judged solely on "number trained," expect shorter trainings and padded registers. Pair output targets with quality and outcome measures, and make it safe to report bad news.
Field practice 6 · Capstone
Using your answers from practices 2–5, assemble a one-page mini M&E plan: results chain (5 lines), 3 indicators with baseline/target/MoV, your riskiest assumption, your data collection methods, and the date of your first quarterly data review. Then take that page into the Navigator — its stages will help you choose tools to support it.
Final knowledge check

Ten questions before you go

1 · What's the key difference between monitoring and evaluation?
Monitoring is continuous tracking of delivery (are we on track?); evaluation is periodic assessment of merit and results (did it work, and why?).
2 · Order the results chain.
Inputs → Activities → Outputs → Outcomes → Impact.
3 · What separates outputs from outcomes?
Control. Outputs are deliverables within the program's control; outcomes are changes in people's behavior or conditions that the program can influence but not guarantee.
4 · What is an assumption in a logframe?
An external condition outside program control that must hold for one results level to lead to the next.
5 · What does SMART stand for?
Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound.
6 · Why is a baseline indispensable?
Change can only be demonstrated against a starting point; without a baseline, an endline value is uninterpretable.
7 · Name the six OECD-DAC criteria.
Relevance, coherence, effectiveness, efficiency, impact, sustainability.
8 · Attribution or contribution: which claim needs a counterfactual?
Attribution — you must show what would have happened without the program (e.g., via a comparison group).
9 · Give two ethical requirements for data collection.
Any two of: informed and voluntary consent; do-no-harm design; confidentiality and secure data handling; collecting only necessary personal data; sharing findings back with communities.
10 · What is adaptive management?
Using monitoring evidence in a regular review rhythm to deliberately adjust program decisions, rather than rigidly executing the original plan.
Reference

Glossary & further reading

Baseline
The value of an indicator before or at the start of an intervention; the comparison point for all later measurement.
Counterfactual
An estimate of what would have happened without the program; the basis for attribution claims.
Disaggregation
Breaking data down by sub-groups (sex, age, disability, location) to reveal differences hidden in averages.
Indicator
An agreed, observable measure used to signal whether a result is occurring.
Logframe
Logical framework: a matrix linking results levels to indicators, means of verification, and assumptions.
Means of verification (MoV)
The concrete data source for an indicator — a register, survey, record, or observation protocol.
Outcome
A change in the behavior, knowledge, or condition of people or institutions resulting from program outputs.
Results chain
The causal sequence inputs → activities → outputs → outcomes → impact.
Theory of change
The explicit logic and assumptions explaining how and why an intervention is expected to produce its results.
Triangulation
Using multiple independent methods or sources to confirm a finding.
